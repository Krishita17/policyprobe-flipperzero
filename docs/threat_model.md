# Threat model, scope and responsible use

PolicyProbe is a defensive assessment tool. Its deliverable is a compliance report, which
is the same artefact a physical-security assessor hands a client at the end of an
engagement. This document sets out what the tool is for, what it will and will not do, and
the conditions under which running it is appropriate.

Author: Krishita Sanjay Choksi

---

## 1. Purpose

To let an assessor answer a question the GRC world asks constantly and has almost no
technical tooling for: *is the physical access control at this site actually capable of
enforcing the access policy written about it?*

An ISO 27001 auditor can confirm that an access control policy exists, that a badge
register is maintained, and that doors have readers on them. What neither the auditor nor
the policy can tell you is whether the credential those readers accept can be reproduced
by anyone who walks past a holder with a reader in their bag. PolicyProbe answers that
question and expresses the answer in the auditor's own language — as evidence for or
against named controls.

## 2. Who this is for

* Internal security and GRC teams assessing their own estate.
* Physical-security assessors working under a signed engagement.
* Auditors and assurance functions who need technical evidence behind a physical-access
  control conclusion.
* Researchers and students studying the gap between physical-layer security and compliance
  frameworks.

## 3. Assessor capability model

The assessor is assumed to have:

* **Written authorisation** for the site and the specific access points in scope.
* **Physical presence** at the site during the assessment window.
* **Possession of credentials they are entitled to hold** — their own issued badge, or
  specimens provided by the client for testing.
* **A Flipper Zero or equivalent multi-protocol tool**, used for reading and observing.

The assessor is *not* assumed to have, and the tool does not help them obtain:

* Credentials belonging to other people.
* Access to systems or spaces outside the engagement scope.
* Any ability to defeat a control that the assessment has not been authorised to test.

## 4. What the tool does

* Reads a credential the assessor is holding and identifies its generation.
* Observes whether the credential exchange authenticates, whether it varies between
  presentations, and whether identifiers appear to be sequentially allocated.
* Attempts a **read-only** authentication against a stock default-key dictionary, where
  the client has authorised that test, to establish whether factory keys remain in place.
* Captures a sub-GHz transmission the assessor themselves triggers, to establish whether
  the code is fixed or rolling.
* Records inventory attributes the assessor observes: reader-to-controller interface,
  tamper supervision, event logging, factor count, product life-cycle status.
* Maps every resulting finding to the ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5
  controls it bears on, and produces a report.

## 5. What the tool deliberately does not do

These are design decisions, not missing features.

* **No credential emulation or writing.** The tool never writes to blank media and never
  orchestrates the Flipper's emulation modes. Establishing that a credential is
  reproducible does not require reproducing it; the finding is that the technology offers
  no protection against duplication, and that finding stands on the identified generation
  and the observed exchange.
* **No brute forcing or key cracking.** The default-key test is a dictionary check of
  published factory defaults. It is not a cryptanalytic attack, and no key-recovery attack
  against a broken cipher is implemented or invoked. Where a generation is known-broken,
  that is reported from the published cryptanalysis, with citations.
* **No jamming.** Rolling-code weaknesses are reported from the encoder family and the
  one-way nature of the exchange. Jam-and-replay is not performed: it is disruptive, it is
  unlawful in most jurisdictions, and it adds nothing to the finding.
* **No relay attacks.**
* **No entry.** The tool produces evidence that a control is not met. It does not open
  doors, and no workflow in this repository ends in a door being opened.
* **No persistence, no implants, no tampering** with client hardware.

The distinction the tool holds to throughout: *an assessment needs evidence that a
weakness exists, not a demonstration of entry.*

## 6. Data handling and repository scope

* All experiments in this repository run on **synthetic facility profiles** generated by
  `src/policyprobe/data/facility.py`, or on the **author's own test media** — blank and
  development tags, a spare fob, an iButton, a remote the author owns.
* The repository contains **no real facility credentials**, no site-specific keys, no
  facility codes, and no card numbers belonging to any organisation.
* Real sample files under `data/real_samples/` carry no facility identifier by design: a
  sample records the technology and the observation, never where it was found. The loader
  refuses any sample not explicitly marked as the author's own device.
* The classifier flags weakness *patterns* — static identifiers, absent cryptography,
  sequential allocation. It is not tuned against any specific commercial product, and the
  broken-cipher list cites published academic cryptanalysis rather than describing how to
  reproduce it.

## 7. Dual-use assessment

Every physical-security assessment tool is dual-use to some degree, and pretending
otherwise would be dishonest. The considered position here:

* The **capabilities** PolicyProbe uses — reading a tag, observing whether a code repeats —
  are present in the stock Flipper firmware and in a hundred tutorials. This project adds
  no offensive capability that did not already exist and is not scarce.
* What this project adds is **the mapping layer**, which is of use only to someone writing
  an assessment. A person seeking unauthorised entry has no use for a control-mapped
  posture score; an assessor has no other way to produce one.
* The deliberate exclusions in section 5 mean the repository is not a shortcut to entry:
  the workflow ends at a report.

The net effect is a tool whose marginal value to a defender is high and whose marginal
value to an attacker is close to zero.

## 8. Legal and ethical requirements

**Do not run this tool against any system you do not own or are not contracted in writing
to assess.** Depending on jurisdiction, probing access control systems without
authorisation may constitute unauthorised access, interception of communications, or
offences under computer-misuse legislation. Radio transmission is separately regulated;
capture and replay of sub-GHz transmissions may be restricted even on equipment you own.

Before any engagement:

1. Obtain written authorisation naming the site, the access points, and the assessment
   window.
2. Agree the test list in advance, including whether the default-key check is in scope.
3. Agree an escalation contact and a stop condition.
4. Agree how findings will be stored and transmitted — a completed PolicyProbe report is a
   list of ways into a building and should be handled accordingly.
5. Confirm local law on radio capture and on access control testing.

This is also the point of ISO/IEC 27001:2022 control `A.8.34` (*Protection of information
systems during audit testing*) and NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 `CA-8` (*Penetration Testing*):
an authorised, scoped, non-disruptive test is itself a control the organisation can
evidence. An unauthorised one is an incident.

## 9. Report handling

A completed report enumerates the weakest ways into a building, ranked. Treat it as the
client's most sensitive document from the engagement:

* Encrypt at rest and in transit.
* Distribute on a need-to-know basis, and record who received it.
* Agree a retention period and destroy it at the end of it.
* Never commit a real report to a public repository. The only report in this repository
  describes a facility that does not exist.
